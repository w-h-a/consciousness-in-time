## 2026-07-01

Finishing up Sebald's *The Rings of Saturn*. It reminds me of the film *Russian Ark*.

Both are a single continuous movement through space that becomes a movement through time. Sokurov's is one unbroken take drifting room to room through eras of the Hermitage, and Sebald's reads like one unbroken take following a narrator on a Suffolk hike. Movement through space becomes movement through time differently in each work. In *Russian Ark* the rooms _are_ the eras. In *The Rings of Saturn* Sebald's space doesn't become another time; the consciousness's attention just summons times and memories and thoughts through free-association.

## 2026-07-02

Not very far into Robinson's *Gilead*, but I immediately thought of Bresson's *Diary of a Country Priest*, although it's been a while since I've watched it.

The whole of Bresson's film is built around a sickly priest's journal, and *Gilead* is Ames's journal/letter to his son as his heart gives out. This might be just superficial, but I'll attend to this some more and maybe rewatch the film again soon. It actually might be better as a book, as much as I love Bresson, this is not my favorite of his. 

Unrelatedly, so far I do like Robinson's prose a bit more than Sebald's but we'll see.

## 2026-07-03

Liking the form of *Gilead* in two senses: (1) it is well written and easier to get into than Sebald's *The Rings of Saturn* and (2) it is a dying man's diary/letter to his son. However, the interesting thing here is that the mode of attention (how Ames perceives the world) is inherently linked to his religion and beliefs and I cannot connect with that at all. Well, I can and have. I am not religious, but something like, say, Malick's *Tree of Life* is one of my favorite movies and Tarkovsky's *The Mirror* feels like a religious experience, but I cannot really connect with the mode of attention in *Gilead*. 

I think the difference is that I can enter doctrine-free transcendence that one finds in Tarkovsky, Ozu, Malick, and some of Dreyer and Bresson. But I have a hard time entering doctrine-bound transcendence. So the films that deal in religious themes that I enjoy elicit something in me without feeling like a sales pitch. But Ames's reverence strikes me as coming from a place that is bound to certain propositions that he believes and that I must sign on to to really get into. The attention is doctrine-bound and that does not jive with me and, indeed, is one of the reasons I prefer Bresson's *A Man Escaped* and *Pickpocket* over his *Diary of a Country Priest*.
