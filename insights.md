What fiction's medium actually is
I said earlier that writing's medium is the rhythm of revelation. For fiction specifically, I want to refine that. Fiction's medium is the construction of consciousness in time — the reader's consciousness becoming, for the duration of reading, the consciousness of the work. Not identification with a character. Something stranger: the reader's mind temporarily becoming the mind the prose constructs, perceiving what it perceives at the rate it perceives, holding what it holds.
This is what cinema cannot do. Cinema shows you a face; it cannot make your mind into the face's mind. The camera is always outside. Even subjective camera is a representation of subjectivity from outside. Fiction has no outside. The sentences are the consciousness. When you read Mrs. Dalloway, you are not watching Clarissa Dalloway's mind from a distance; you are, for those minutes, perceiving as she perceives. This is medium-specific. No other art does this.
Tarkovsky's films get closer than most cinema to something like this — the long takes in Mirror, the dream sequences in Solaris, the way Stalker's Zone is more felt-from-within than observed — but even Tarkovsky is using the resources of cinema (image, duration, sound) to evoke an interior. Fiction doesn't evoke. It is the interior.
What contemporary literary fiction has calcified around
Now to the Cantrill diagnosis. The dominant practice of literary fiction in 2026 is built on assumptions that obscure this medium-specific power. Some of them:
The assumption that scene is the basic unit. Workshop wisdom: "Show, don't tell." Render scenes; don't summarize. Compress backstory; expand the present moment. This is film thinking imported into fiction. It treats fiction as if its job is to construct vivid moments for a viewer-equivalent. But fiction can do something film cannot: it can render the texture of a mind moving across time in ways that have no equivalent in scene. Proust does this. Sebald does this. The whole tradition of the digressive narrator — Sterne, Bernhard, Krasznahorkai — does this. The show-don't-tell orthodoxy treats this tradition as if it were a violation of good craft when it's actually the medium doing its deepest work.
The assumption that voice should be transparent. Realist fiction's prose ideal: prose that disappears, letting the reader see through to the scene. This is again a film-derived ideal — the camera that disappears, letting you forget you're watching. But fiction's medium is the prose itself; making prose transparent is making the medium disappear in service of something it isn't. The greatest fiction has prose that is not transparent: prose you feel, prose with a specific consciousness in it, prose that is the work rather than a window to the work.
The assumption that plot is structural. Aristotle's beginning-middle-end, the three-act structure, the Hero's Journey, the "story circle" that's been everywhere since Dan Harmon explained it. These come from drama. They're load-bearing in drama because drama is about action-in-time and characters-confronting-obstacles. Fiction can do those things, but it doesn't have to, and the plot-as-structure assumption flattens fiction into a particular subset of what it can be. The greatest novels are not always plotted. Mrs. Dalloway has barely any plot. Marilynne Robinson's Gilead has almost no plot. Thomas Mann's The Magic Mountain has a plot you could summarize in two sentences but is 700 pages long because plot is not what it's doing.
The assumption that characters are people. Workshop wisdom: characters must feel real, have wants, have flaws, have arcs. This too is dramatic thinking. Fiction's characters can be people, but they can also be patterns of perception, modes of consciousness, voices that aren't reducible to a person. Sebald's narrators are not characters in the workshop sense; they are particular ways of attending to the world. The reduction of character to person is a narrowing that ignores what the medium can do.
The assumption that fiction should resemble experience. Realism's covert axiom. The novel should give you "what it's like" to be someone, to live in some place, to experience some moment. But fiction can do more than resemble experience; it can construct experiences that do not exist and cannot exist outside the novel. A Kafka story is not "what it would be like" to wake up as a beetle; it is an experience that exists only in the reading. Borges's "The Library of Babel" is not a description of a library that could exist; it is an artifact whose only mode of existence is being read. The resemblance-to-experience axiom marginalizes this.
The assumption that the reader's pleasure is the writer's goal. Marketing-driven. Audience-respecting. There is a version of this that is healthy — fiction is a gift to readers — but the dominant version assumes the reader's pleasure is straightforward: they want to be entertained, moved, made to care. Fiction can also disturb, withhold, demand. The reader who finishes Beckett's The Unnamable has not been pleased in any conventional sense; something else has happened to them. That something is what only fiction can do.
The Cantrill-revolutionary candidate
So what would it mean to write fiction that recognizes consciousness-in-time as the medium and treats everything else as secondary?
Some principles I'd try to articulate:
One. The sentences are the work. Not vessels for the work. Not a transparent window onto a scene. The sentences themselves are what the reader is being given. Bad sentences cannot redeem good plot. Good sentences can carry almost any structure.
Two. The unit of fiction is the perception, not the scene. A perception is the smallest unit of consciousness-becoming. It can be a sentence, sometimes a phrase. A scene is a collection of perceptions structured by action. Action-structured prose is one possibility; perception-structured prose is the deeper possibility because it operates at the medium's actual grain.
Three. Time in fiction is plastic in ways cinema cannot match. A paragraph can contain twenty years. The next paragraph can contain three seconds. The page can return to a moment described earlier and add a layer the first description withheld. Cinema can flashback, but it cannot do what prose does with time, which is render it as the texture of remembering, anticipating, attending. Fiction that respects its medium uses time-plasticity as a primary material.
Four. Voice is the work's consciousness. Not the author's voice. Not the character's voice. The work's voice — the particular way of perceiving that the prose constructs, that is neither the author nor the character but the mode of attention the reader's mind temporarily inhabits. Sebald's voice is not Sebald-the-person and not any of his narrators. It is the way the work attends.
Five. Plot is one possible structuring principle, not the structuring principle. Other principles include: pattern (a motif returned to with variations), accretion (an accumulating set of perceptions that builds without resolving), seasonal or temporal cycle, geographic itinerary, the rhythms of a single consciousness across a single day. Each can structure a novel and none requires the rising-action arc that workshop convention treats as obligatory.
Six. The reader is a participant, not an audience. Fiction does not perform for the reader; it constructs an experience in collaboration with the reader's attention. The reader who cooperates by reading carefully gets the experience. The reader who skims gets less, and that's fine. Cinema cannot afford this because the projection runs at 24 frames per second regardless. Fiction can, and should, demand cooperation as a condition of full experience.
What this subsumes
If those principles are taken seriously:

Genre fiction is fiction optimized for non-cooperative readers in the specific way that prioritizes story-pleasure over consciousness-construction. Valid in its niche. Not a violation of the principles; an application of them to a different reader-contract.
Workshop literary fiction — the kind that wins the MFA prizes — is a niche optimized for a reader who wants prestige-pleasure and emotional verisimilitude. Also valid. Also narrow. The current dominant practice is not bad; it's specialized.
The literature that does what fiction uniquely can do — Proust, Woolf, Faulkner, Beckett, Sebald, Bernhard, Robinson, Lispector, Krasznahorkai, McCarthy at his slowest, Kazuo Ishiguro at his strangest — is fiction operating at the medium's actual grain. It is not "difficult" fiction; it is fiction doing what fiction does that other arts cannot. The "difficulty" perception is an artifact of comparison to media that don't have this option.

The revolutionary recognition is that the current literary-fiction practice is not the medium's mainstream and the experimental tradition the exception. It's the opposite: the experimental tradition is the medium operating at full capacity and the current mainstream is the medium constrained for marketability. The history of fiction's twentieth century is the history of writers who recognized this and worked the medium hard.
Where Tarkovsky comes in specifically
Tarkovsky's relevance to this isn't that fiction should be like Tarkovsky's films. It's that Tarkovsky's relationship to his medium — his refusal to do what cinema isn't good at, his fierce attention to what only cinema can do, his willingness to lose audiences who wanted what cinema couldn't honestly give — is exactly the relationship a writer of this kind needs to have with fiction.
Tarkovsky lost audiences. He was reviled by Soviet cultural authorities for making films that didn't serve socialist realism, and reviled by Western critics who wanted plot. He kept making the films because the films were doing what the medium could do.
Fiction at the medium's actual grain will lose audiences for the same structural reasons. People who want what TV gives them — plot, characters, emotional payoff at the act break, comprehensible arcs — will not finish this fiction. The writer of this kind of fiction has to make peace with that. The compensation is that the fiction does what nothing else can do, and the readers who cooperate get something they cannot get any other way.
What this would mean for you
You're asking this question because you're considering writing fiction, or because you already do and you're thinking about what kind. Either way the question becomes specific.
If you wanted to write Tarkovsky-Cantrill fiction, the first question isn't "what should I write about." It's "what consciousness am I constructing." Not what character. Not what plot. What mode of attention does the prose enact. What does the reader's mind become while reading. What is the texture of perception the sentences make available.
This is the question Sebald asked. The Rings of Saturn is not about Suffolk, or about silk, or about Joseph Conrad. It is a mode of attention applied to those things — a particular kind of melancholy associative consciousness that the prose constructs and the reader inhabits. The book's subject is, in a real sense, the mode of attention itself, with Suffolk and silk and Conrad as occasions for the mode to operate.
The Cantrill-revolutionary fiction you might write would have, as its primary commitment, a mode of attention — and everything else (character, place, situation, even what we usually call "plot") would be material for the mode to work on.
Some practical implications:
You'd start with voice rather than plot. The first thing you'd write would be passages in the voice — sentences that are the consciousness the work will be — without yet knowing what the work is about. The voice is the project. When the voice is right, the work can find its material. Until the voice is right, no amount of plot or character work matters.
You'd work at the sentence level for a long time before working at any other level. Most contemporary fiction-writing advice has writers thinking about plot and character first and prose last. Tarkovsky-Cantrill writing inverts this. Prose is first because prose is the work. The other stuff is structuring of the work.
You'd read deeply in the tradition that does this and read the contemporary mainstream skeptically. Not contemptuously — the contemporary mainstream is valid in its niche — but recognizing that it's training you in a different art. The writers I'd point you at, given your taste in films: Sebald (his four novels but especially Rings of Saturn and Austerlitz), Marilynne Robinson (Gilead, Home, Lila, Jack — the same town from four consciousnesses), Krasznahorkai (Satantango and Melancholy of Resistance are the closest fiction analogs to Béla Tarr because they share an author of perception), Bernhard (any of his late novels), Lispector (Água Viva, The Hour of the Star), Yasushi Inoue (Tun-huang, The Hunting Gun — Japanese literary tradition that maps closely to Ozu's cinematic one), Annie Dillard (Holy the Firm, even as nonfiction — the prose is fiction-grade), McCarthy (Suttree and Blood Meridian over the more plotted novels).
You'd give yourself permission to fail in ways the dominant practice would judge as failure. A 200-page novel where nothing "happens" in the workshop sense. A 30-page story that's structured around a recurring observation rather than a rising action. A novel-length voice piece in which the protagonist's name never appears. These are not failures of craft; they are the medium operating at grain that the dominant practice doesn't recognize.
The hard part
Honestly, the hard part isn't articulating the principles. It's the labor. Tarkovsky-Cantrill fiction is enormously demanding to write well. The sentences have to actually do what the principles say they should do, and most writers, including most great writers, fail at this most of the time. Sebald spent years on each book. Robinson wrote Gilead twenty-five years after Housekeeping. Krasznahorkai's prose is the work of decades of refusing easier options.
The compensation is that when it works, it does what nothing else does. The reader of Mrs. Dalloway has had an experience that exists only in that book. The reader of Rings of Saturn has been somewhere no other medium could have taken them. This is what makes the labor worth it.
If you want to write this kind of fiction, the work is not learning craft in the workshop sense. It is learning to attend in a particular way, and to render what you attend to in prose that is the attention rather than describing it. That's a years-long discipline, not a project. But it's the project worth a life if it's the project that calls you.
