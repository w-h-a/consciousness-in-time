# Aim Notes — Background

The expanded reasoning behind the CLAUDE.md's "Aim" and "Standards" sections. Deliberation-background, not an operating document. The CLAUDE.md is the lean standard; this is the fuller argument it was distilled from.

## What fiction's medium actually is

Writing's medium is the rhythm of revelation. For fiction specifically: fiction's medium is the construction of consciousness in time — the reader's consciousness becoming, for the duration of reading, the consciousness of the work. Not identification with a character. Something stranger: the reader's mind temporarily becoming the mind the prose constructs, perceiving what it perceives at the rate it perceives, holding what it holds.

This is what cinema cannot do. Cinema shows you a face; it cannot make your mind into the face's mind. The camera is always outside. Even subjective camera is a representation of subjectivity from outside. Fiction has no outside. The sentences are the consciousness. When you read *Mrs. Dalloway*, you are not watching Clarissa Dalloway's mind from a distance; you are, for those minutes, perceiving as she perceives. This is medium-specific. No other art does this.

Tarkovsky's films get closer than most cinema — the long takes in *Mirror*, the dream sequences in *Solaris*, the way *Stalker*'s Zone is more felt-from-within than observed — but even Tarkovsky is using the resources of cinema (image, duration, sound) to evoke an interior. Fiction doesn't evoke. It is the interior.

## What contemporary literary fiction has calcified around

The dominant practice of literary fiction is built on assumptions that obscure this medium-specific power.

**The assumption that scene is the basic unit.** "Show, don't tell." Render scenes; don't summarize. This is film thinking imported into fiction. But fiction can render the texture of a mind moving across time in ways that have no equivalent in scene. Proust does this. Sebald does this. The digressive-narrator tradition — Sterne, Bernhard, Krasznahorkai — does this. Show-don't-tell treats this as a violation of craft when it's the medium doing its deepest work.

**The assumption that voice should be transparent.** Prose that disappears, letting the reader see through to the scene — another film-derived ideal, the camera that disappears. But fiction's medium is the prose itself; making it transparent makes the medium disappear in service of something it isn't. The greatest fiction has prose you feel, prose with a specific consciousness in it, prose that is the work rather than a window to it.

**The assumption that plot is structural.** Beginning-middle-end, three-act, the Hero's Journey, the story circle. These come from drama, where they're load-bearing because drama is action-in-time and characters-confronting-obstacles. Fiction can do those things but doesn't have to. *Mrs. Dalloway* has barely any plot. *Gilead* has almost none. *The Magic Mountain* has a two-sentence plot across 700 pages because plot is not what it's doing.

**The assumption that characters are people.** Characters must feel real, have wants, flaws, arcs — dramatic thinking. Fiction's characters can be people, but also patterns of perception, modes of consciousness, voices not reducible to a person. Sebald's narrators are particular ways of attending to the world. Reducing character to person is a narrowing.

**The assumption that fiction should resemble experience.** Realism's covert axiom: give you "what it's like." But fiction can construct experiences that do not and cannot exist outside the novel. Kafka's beetle is not "what it would be like"; it is an experience that exists only in the reading. Borges's "Library of Babel" is an artifact whose only mode of existence is being read.

**The assumption that the reader's pleasure is the writer's goal.** There's a healthy version — fiction is a gift — but the dominant version assumes pleasure is straightforward: entertain, move, make care. Fiction can also disturb, withhold, demand. The reader who finishes *The Unnamable* has not been pleased; something else has happened to them. That something is what only fiction can do.

## The principles

1. **The sentences are the work.** Not vessels. Not a transparent window. The sentences themselves are what the reader is given. Bad sentences cannot redeem good plot. Good sentences can carry almost any structure.
2. **The unit is the perception, not the scene.** A perception is the smallest unit of consciousness-becoming — a sentence, sometimes a phrase. A scene is perceptions structured by action. Perception-structured prose operates at the medium's actual grain.
3. **Time is plastic.** A paragraph can contain twenty years; the next, three seconds. The page can return to a moment and add a layer the first description withheld. Cinema can flashback but cannot render time as the texture of remembering, anticipating, attending. Time-plasticity is a primary material.
4. **Voice is the work's consciousness.** Not the author's, not the character's — the particular way of perceiving the prose constructs, the mode of attention the reader's mind temporarily inhabits. Sebald's voice is not Sebald-the-person and not his narrators. It is the way the work attends.
5. **Plot is one structuring principle among many.** Pattern (a motif returned to with variation), accretion (perceptions building without resolving), seasonal or temporal cycle, geographic itinerary, the rhythms of a single consciousness across a day. None requires the rising-action arc.
6. **The reader is a participant, not an audience.** Fiction constructs an experience in collaboration with the reader's attention. The cooperating reader gets the experience; the skimming reader gets less, and that's fine. Cinema can't afford this — 24 frames a second regardless. Fiction can, and should, demand cooperation as a condition of full experience.

## What this subsumes

- **Genre fiction** is optimized for non-cooperative readers, prioritizing story-pleasure over consciousness-construction. Valid in its niche. An application of the principles to a different reader-contract, not a violation.
- **Workshop literary fiction** — the MFA-prize kind — is optimized for a reader wanting prestige-pleasure and emotional verisimilitude. Valid. Narrow. Not bad; specialized.
- **The literature that does what fiction uniquely can do** — Proust, Woolf, Faulkner, Beckett, Sebald, Bernhard, Robinson, Lispector, Krasznahorkai, McCarthy at his slowest, Ishiguro at his strangest — is fiction at the medium's actual grain. Not "difficult"; doing what other arts cannot. The "difficulty" is an artifact of comparison to media that lack this option.

The revolutionary recognition: current literary-fiction practice is not the medium's mainstream with the experimental tradition as exception. It's the opposite. The experimental tradition is the medium at full capacity; the mainstream is the medium constrained for marketability. The twentieth century of fiction is the history of writers who recognized this and worked the medium hard.

## Where Tarkovsky comes in

Not that fiction should resemble his films. That his relationship to his medium — refusing what cinema isn't good at, attending fiercely to what only cinema can do, accepting the loss of audiences who wanted what cinema couldn't honestly give — is the relationship this kind of writer needs to fiction.

Tarkovsky lost audiences. Reviled by Soviet authorities for films that didn't serve socialist realism, and by Western critics who wanted plot. He kept making them because they did what the medium could do.

Fiction at the medium's grain will lose audiences for the same structural reasons. People who want what TV gives — plot, characters, act-break payoff, comprehensible arcs — will not finish it. The writer makes peace with that. The compensation: it does what nothing else can, and the cooperating reader gets something unavailable any other way.

## What this means in practice

The first question isn't "what should I write about." It's "what consciousness am I constructing." Not what character, not what plot. What mode of attention does the prose enact; what does the reader's mind become; what texture of perception do the sentences make available.

This is the question Sebald asked. *The Rings of Saturn* is not about Suffolk, or silk, or Conrad. It is a mode of attention applied to those things — a melancholy associative consciousness the prose constructs and the reader inhabits. The subject is the mode of attention itself, with Suffolk and silk and Conrad as occasions for it to operate.

- **Start with voice, not plot.** The first thing written is passages in the voice — sentences that are the consciousness the work will be — without yet knowing what it's about. The voice is the project. When it's right, the work finds its material.
- **Work at the sentence level long before any other level.** Most advice puts plot and character first, prose last. This inverts it. Prose is first because prose is the work.
- **Read the tradition deeply and the mainstream skeptically** — not contemptuously; it's a valid different art.
- **Give permission to fail by the dominant practice's judgment.** A 200-page novel where nothing "happens." A 30-page story built on a recurring observation. A voice-piece where the protagonist's name never appears. Not failures of craft — the medium at a grain the dominant practice doesn't recognize.

## The hard part

Not articulating the principles. The labor. This fiction is enormously demanding to write well; the sentences have to actually do what the principles say, and most writers, most of the time, fail. Sebald spent years on each book. Robinson wrote *Gilead* twenty-five years after *Housekeeping*. Krasznahorkai's prose is decades of refusing easier options.

The compensation: when it works, it does what nothing else does. The reader of *Mrs. Dalloway* has had an experience that exists only in that book. The reader of *Rings of Saturn* has been somewhere no other medium could take them.

The work is not learning craft in the workshop sense. It is learning to attend in a particular way, and to render what you attend to in prose that is the attention rather than describing it. A years-long discipline, not a project. The project worth a life, if it's the one that calls.
